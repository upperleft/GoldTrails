Gold After a Flood

High water can rearrange a familiar river. Once access is safe and open, start with what changed, then let small, comparable samples guide the next visit.

By Paul Shoemaker · Research and writing with assistance from ChatGPT · Published November 19, 2026

The bend is still there. The big outcrop looks familiar. But the little gravel pocket that gave you gold last time may have disappeared beneath fresh sand, been scoured away, or shifted with the channel. Returning after a flood means reading the place again.

Freshly exposed gravel invites a closer look. Plan the return around two questions: what has the river changed, and which of those changes can you safely test?

Wait for a safe return

This guide concerns a later visit after flooding, when the area is open and a safe approach is available. Stay out of floodwater, avoid disaster areas, and respect road and trail closures. Falling water does not clear a damaged site for recreation.{{cite:nws-after}}{{cite:nws-during}}

What a flood can change

A river carries sediment as well as water. USGS explains that faster water can move larger particles more readily, while sediment can settle where flow slows and later be picked up again. A storm can therefore rearrange the material you were sampling, rather than simply add a fresh layer on top.{{cite:usgs-sediment}}

Gold-bearing streams add another sorting problem. The Forest Service and BLM panning guide describes gold settling through agitated gravel toward bedrock or an impermeable clay layer, and collecting in suitable pockets and crevices. It also identifies bends, bar heads, and places where flow changes as search clues. These are possible sampling positions, not promises.{{cite:usfs-blm}}

High water may expose a useful surface, bury one, or redistribute material around it. Give a newly cleaned crack and a fresh gravel sheet different labels in your notebook, then sample each separately to find out what it holds.

Before returning

Check local alerts, route conditions, closures, and the forecast for the upstream drainage. USGS notes that a river can rise from rain far upstream, and that larger rivers may take days to rise and fall. A dry sky overhead is only one part of the picture.{{cite:usgs-streamflow}}

A relevant USGS streamgage can help you follow the trend. Record the station and observation time, and remember that a gauge does not inspect your access route or establish safe conditions at your sampling spot.

Recheck permission for the exact location and activity. On BLM lands, collecting on an existing mining claim requires the claimholder’s permission; local restrictions also matter. Other land managers and jurisdictions have their own rules. Ask the responsible office when access or permitted methods are uncertain.{{cite:blm-access}}

Once there, stay back from undercut banks, unstable trees, and damaged crossings. Do not dig into banks to follow a promising layer. The Forest Service guide specifically warns that undercut banks can collapse with trees and boulders. Choose an observation point and any later sampling position you can reach from stable ground without entering moving water.{{cite:usfs-blm}}

Read the reach again

Before opening the pan, compare the reach with old photographs or a simple sketch. Has the channel moved toward a bank? Is formerly exposed bedrock covered? Has a bar grown, split, or lost its upstream end? Note fresh deposits and exposed surfaces separately. If you have no before photograph, write “newly noticed,” rather than assuming “newly formed.”

Pick a small comparison you can complete without disturbing banks or vegetation. For example, compare a safely accessible patch of fresh surface gravel with an adjacent older surface, or loose material beside an exposed bedrock pocket with material from the pocket itself where collection is permitted. Keep each position and layer separate.

Use the shape of the ground to pose a question. Does the fresh gravel contain more visible gold than the nearby comparison? Does the exposed pocket repeat a promising result? Black sand, large cobbles, or an attractive bend can help frame the question. Their appearance does not answer it. Our guide to where gold settles gives more context for choosing a test.

Make a useful comparison

The following is Gold Trails’ suggested field exercise. It is a way to improve a small comparison, not a resource estimate or a standardized recovery test.

  1. Label the positions first. Give each sample an ID and describe the surface or layer. Keep a sketch or photograph reference. Avoid mixing fresh surface gravel with deeper material in one sample.
  2. Keep the amount comparable. Use the same measured starting volume and classifier opening. Record whether volume means original gravel or material passing the screen. Wash and inspect oversize rather than assuming it contains nothing useful.
  3. Keep the recovery method steady. Use the same pan, preparation, and careful cleanup. Retain concentrates and checking material separately. Changing technique between positions can change what you recover.
  4. Repeat the comparison. Take additional, separately labeled samples from each position. Natural gold is unevenly distributed; equal volumes need not contain equal gold. One unusually good pan is a reason to investigate nearby, not a reason to describe the whole bar as rich.
  5. Record the empty pans. Write down no visible gold, uncertain identification, and incomplete samples as carefully as a promising find. Counts of mixed-size specks are not grams, and a blank pan does not establish that the deposit contains no gold.

Compare results only after checking what changed between samples. For more on keeping both the successful and disappointing observations useful, see How to Compare Test Pans Without Fooling Yourself.

A flood-return notebook

Use one line per sample, with a separate note explaining the decision to return. Keep precise locations private where appropriate.

What to keep with each comparison
RecordUseful detail
Visit and accessDate, alert and closure checks, permission, route condition, and reason to proceed or cancel.
Water and changeGauge station and time if used; photograph reference; observed erosion, burial, or fresh sediment.
SampleID, position, layer, starting volume, screen opening, and recovery method.
ResultVisible gold or zero, identification uncertainty, repeat samples, and material retained for checking.

A good return visit can end with a better sketch and no gold. If a result does repeat, you have a clearer next question. If it does not, you have learned something useful without turning the whole bar over.
